The Solo Traveler's Guide to the Faroe Islands: Chasing Waterfalls and Solitude in the North Atlantic

2026-07-20 · Destinations · Tags: solotravelguide, The

There are places that feel like they belong to another world, and the Faroe Islands are exactly that—a jagged archipelago of 18 volcanic islands rising from the North Atlantic, where sheep outnumber people, and the weather changes faster than a mood ring. For solo travelers, this is not just a destination; it's a pilgrimage to the edge of the map. Here, you won't find crowded beaches or bustling nightlife. Instead, you'll discover a raw, untamed landscape that demands your presence and rewards your courage. If you're seeking a journey that strips away the noise of modern life and leaves you with nothing but wind, sea, and your own thoughts, the Faroe Islands are calling. This guide will show you how to navigate this Nordic wonderland alone—safely, meaningfully, and with a sense of wonder that will stay with you long after you've returned home.

Why the Faroe Islands Are a Solo Traveler's Dream

The Faroe Islands are not a typical solo travel destination. They're not on the backpacker trail, and they don't have a hostel culture like Southeast Asia. But that's precisely their appeal. As a solo traveler, you'll find a rare kind of freedom here—the freedom to hike a mountain trail without encountering another soul, to stand at a cliff's edge with only the sound of puffins and waves, and to experience a place that still feels undiscovered. The islands are incredibly safe, with one of the lowest crime rates in the world. The locals, known as Faroese, are warm and curious, often eager to share stories about their homeland. And because the tourism infrastructure is still developing, you'll find that the few travelers you do meet are kindred spirits—people who chose this remote corner of the world for its authenticity, not its Instagram fame.

What makes the Faroe Islands particularly special for solo adventurers is the sense of scale. The entire country is roughly the size of a small city, with a population of just over 50,000. This means you can cover a surprising amount of ground in a short time, yet each island feels distinct. You can base yourself in the capital, Tórshavn, and take day trips to different islands, or you can rent a car and create your own loop. The lack of crowds means you'll often have entire valleys, waterfalls, and villages to yourself—a luxury that's becoming increasingly rare in the world of travel.

Getting There and Getting Around: Practicalities for the Lone Explorer

Reaching the Faroe Islands is easier than you might think. Atlantic Airways and Scandinavian Airlines offer direct flights from Copenhagen, Reykjavik, and Edinburgh, with flight times ranging from 1.5 to 2.5 hours. If you're coming from North America, you can fly via Iceland or Denmark. The islands are also accessible by ferry from Denmark (Smyril Line), which takes about 36 hours and is an adventure in itself. For solo travelers, flying is the most efficient option, but the ferry offers a unique way to arrive by sea, with dramatic views of the islands as you approach.

Once you're there, the best way to explore is by car. Renting a car gives you the flexibility to stop at every scenic overlook, chase the ever-changing weather, and reach remote trailheads. The roads are well-maintained, and the undersea tunnels (including the recent Eysturoyartunnin, which features a roundabout deep beneath the sea) make island-hopping a breeze. If you're not comfortable driving, there are public buses that connect the main towns, but they run infrequently, especially on Sundays. For solo travelers, I highly recommend renting a car—it's your ticket to independence. Just be prepared for narrow roads, single-lane tunnels, and the occasional sheep crossing. Driving here is part of the adventure, and you'll quickly get used to pulling over to let faster cars pass.

Another option is to join a guided tour, which can be a great way to meet other travelers and learn about the islands' history and culture. Many tour operators offer day trips to the most iconic spots, like the Mulafossur Waterfall or the cliffs of Vestmanna. But for the true solo experience, I suggest a mix: rent a car for a few days to explore on your own, and then join a boat tour or a guided hike to connect with others and gain deeper insights.

Where to Stay and How to Find Community on the Islands

Accommodation in the Faroe Islands ranges from cozy guesthouses to modern hotels, but the most authentic experience is staying in a traditional Faroese home. Many locals rent out rooms or apartments through platforms like Airbnb, and this is a fantastic way to get a feel for everyday life. As a solo traveler, you'll appreciate the warmth of a host who can point you to hidden gems and share local stories. In Tórshavn, you'll find a handful of hotels, but for a more intimate stay, look for guesthouses in villages like Gjógv, Saksun, or Eiði. These small communities offer a glimpse into the Faroese way of life, and you'll often be the only visitor, which makes for memorable interactions.

Meeting other travelers in the Faroe Islands requires a bit of effort, but it's far from impossible. The tourism scene is small, so you'll likely run into the same faces if you're out and about. Hostels are rare, but there are a few, like the Giljanes Hostel in Vágar, which is a converted lighthouse—an unforgettable place to stay. Another option is to join a group activity, such as a photography workshop, a kayaking tour, or a birdwatching excursion. These are excellent ways to bond with fellow travelers who share your interests. Additionally, the Faroese are incredibly social, and you might find yourself invited to a local gathering or a 'kvøldseta' (a traditional evening get-together). Don't be shy—accept the invitation. The solo traveler's community here is built on genuine connections, not just shared itineraries.

Top Experiences for the Solo Adventurer: From Waterfalls to Cliffside Hikes

The Faroe Islands are a playground for outdoor enthusiasts, and as a solo traveler, you have the luxury of setting your own pace. Here are some experiences that are particularly rewarding when you're on your own:

  • Hike to the Mulafossur Waterfall in Vágar: This iconic waterfall cascades directly into the ocean, and the view from the cliff above is nothing short of breathtaking. The hike is short and easy, making it a perfect solo excursion. Go early in the morning to have the place to yourself.
  • Take a boat tour through the Vestmanna Bird Cliffs: While this is a group activity, it's a must-do. The boat weaves through narrow sea stacks and caves, with thousands of seabirds swirling overhead. As a solo traveler, you'll be free to soak in the spectacle without distractions.
  • Explore the village of Gjógv: This tiny village on the island of Eysturoy is famous for its natural harbor, carved into the cliffs. Wander the colorful houses, hike up to the viewpoint, and enjoy a quiet coffee at the local café. It's a place that feels like a secret, even though it's on every itinerary.
  • Drive the road to Saksun: This remote village is nestled in a valley with a dramatic lagoon and a turf-roofed church. The drive itself is an adventure, with winding roads and panoramic views. Once there, you can hike along the coast or simply sit and listen to the silence.
  • Climb the summit of Slættaratindur: At 880 meters, this is the highest peak in the Faroe Islands. The hike is challenging but manageable for a fit solo traveler, and the reward is a 360-degree view of the entire archipelago. On a clear day, you can see all the way to Iceland.

Each of these experiences offers a chance to connect with nature and yourself. The Faroe Islands have a way of making you feel both insignificant and empowered at the same time—a humbling reminder of the world's vastness and your own resilience.

Safety and Practical Tips for the Solo Wanderer

Safety is a top concern for any solo traveler, and the Faroe Islands are one of the safest places on Earth. Violent crime is virtually nonexistent, and the biggest risks are weather-related. The climate is unpredictable, with rain, wind, and fog possible at any time, even in summer. Always check the forecast before heading out, and be prepared for sudden changes. Wear layers, waterproof clothing, and sturdy hiking boots. The trails can be muddy and slippery, so take your time and don't be afraid to turn back if conditions worsen.

Another practical tip: the Faroe Islands are expensive. As a solo traveler, you'll be paying for accommodation and meals on your own, so budget accordingly. To save money, consider staying in guesthouses with kitchen access and cooking some of your own meals. Grocery stores like Bónus and Miklagarður are affordable, and you can stock up on local ingredients like lamb, fish, and rye bread. Also, be aware that many restaurants close early, especially outside Tórshavn, so plan your meals ahead.

When it comes to connectivity, you'll find that mobile coverage is surprisingly good, even in remote areas. But don't rely on it for navigation—download offline maps before you go. The Faroese are also known for their helpfulness, so don't hesitate to ask for directions or advice. And finally, respect the environment. The Faroe Islands are fragile, and the locals are passionate about preserving their land. Stick to marked trails, leave no trace, and always close gates behind you to keep sheep where they belong.

Conclusion

The Faroe Islands are not a destination for everyone. They're for the traveler who finds beauty in gray skies, who sees adventure in a winding road, and who understands that solitude is not loneliness but a chance to hear your own thoughts. As a solo traveler, you'll find a rare kind of peace here—a peace that comes from standing on a cliff, watching the Atlantic crash below, and realizing that you are exactly where you need to be. The islands will challenge you, but they will also reward you with moments of pure, unadulterated wonder. So pack your rain jacket, rent that car, and let the Faroe Islands show you what it means to be truly alone—and truly alive.
